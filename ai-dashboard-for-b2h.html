★ CASE FILE #007 ★ UX Research AI Strategy

Teaching a booking flow to stop losing people at step 3.

A mixed-methods deep dive into why enterprise users were abandoning their carts right before checkout—and how fixing a mental model mismatch saved $120k in dropped revenue.

MY ROLE Lead UX Researcher
TIMELINE 6 Weeks (Q2 2026)
TEAM SIZE 1 Researcher, 2 Designers, 3 Devs
TOOLKIT / METHODS Usability Testing, LLM Prompting, Figma
Figure 1.0: Final interactive prototype showing the overhauled step 3 mental model.
⚡ THE QUICK RECEIPTS (TL;DR)

The Problem: 42% of users abandoned the SaaS booking flow on the confirmation page because hidden authorization fees triggered panic.
What I Did: Conducted 8 moderated usability interviews, mapped out the team's flawed mental model assumptions, and rapid-prototyped a transparent fee breakdown.
Headline Outcome: Reduced task confusion from 6/8 participants to 1/8 in round 2 testing and cut checkout drop-offs by 28% post-launch.

01. The Scene // "Houston, we have a leak"

Every Monday morning, support tickets came in with some version of the same panicked sentence: "I clicked complete, but I think I just got charged twice and I don't know why." Nobody on the product team could tell me how often it was actually happening. All we knew was that step 3 had a drop-off graph that looked like a cliff.

The brief was originally given to me as "Make the button green and fix the copy." But design problems are rarely just button colors. I decided to dig into what users were actually thinking when they hit that page.

"I felt like the interface was trying to trick me into a subscription. I just backed out." — Participant #4, Moderated Usability Session

02. What I Actually Did // "Doing the real homework"

I bypassed the assumptions and structured a 3-part research methodology focused on uncovering mental model mismatches.

  • Research Questions: What specific visual cues cause trust breakdown at step 3? How do users calculate risk before hitting "Confirm"?
  • Sample Size & Demographics: 8 participants (4 prospective buyers, 4 existing customers upgrading plans) recruited via UserTesting.
  • Methods Used: 45-minute think-aloud moderated usability sessions, followed by a card-sorting exercise to organize pricing priorities.
  • Synthesis Method: Affinity mapping in FigJam to code emotional friction points (Confusion, Fear, Friction).
Figure 2.1: Affinity map clustering participant pain points into 3 primary themes.
Figure 2.2: Card sorting results revealing users wanted fee breakdowns earlier.

03. The Messy Middle // "When my first prototype failed"

My first low-fidelity wireframe tested amazingly—right up until I realized I had accidentally biased the test. I had shown it to internal team members who already understood our internal tax glossary!

⚠️
The Pivot: When I tested the exact same wireframe with 4 brand-new external participants during Round 2, 3 of them got stuck on the term "Authorization Hold." They thought it was a hidden penalty fee. I had to scrap the entire modal and redesign the transparency hierarchy from scratch.

04. The Fix // "Designing with receipts"

Every pixel in the final design tied directly back to a research finding from our synthesis phase.

Decision 01: Upfront Pricing Guarantee

Backed by finding: 7/8 users expressed anxiety about "hidden fees." We added an explicit price breakdown card before credit card entry.

Decision 02: Progressive Disclosure Tooltips

Backed by finding: Replacing jargon with micro-tooltips reduced support ticket inquiries related to billing terms down to zero during testing.

BEFORE: Cluttered layout with hidden fee language.
AFTER: Clean hierarchy with upfront authorization breakdown.

05. Did It Work? // "The scoreboard"

1/8 Task confusion in validation testing (down from 6/8)
+28% Checkout completion rate post-launch
-45% Reduction in billing-related support tickets

Beyond the quantitative metrics, the qualitative victory was clear: during final validation tests, participants stopped pausing at step 3 altogether. They sailed right through to payment confirmation without asking a single clarification question.

06. Behind the Scenes // "Director's Commentary"

If I were doing this project again today, I would involve the engineering team in the second round of user interviews. We lost 3 days debating whether progressive disclosure was technically feasible when a simple CSS accordion could have solved it immediately. I learned that showing devs a 30-second clip of a user struggling is 10x more effective than writing a 10-page spec document.
